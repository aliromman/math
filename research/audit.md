# Mathematical publication review

This review covers all 104 mathematical notes on `main` through PR 118. It compares the source questions with the Lean definitions, theorem statements and proofs, or with the finite search bounds for the two partial investigations. Each entry links the underlying research record. Published articles and revised preprints take precedence for statement numbering. A source can ask a wider question than the theorem proved here.

The reviewers checked quantifiers, degenerate cases, equality and uniqueness clauses, the definitions of finite witnesses, and the links between a computational model and the original object. They repeated selected computations independently and checked forward citations and related formulations. An additional pass compared the earlier October catalogue and manuscript collection. Database coverage and access to papers vary. A report that a search found no earlier solution records the outcome of that search. Global priority remains undetermined. ## 1. Disproof of the Engbers–Erey conjecture on maximum average maximal-matching size in trees of fixed order and diameter

**Disproof.** Sui–Li 1.2 specifies both parity cases and uniqueness up to isomorphism. In the odd/odd case (15,11) one leaf move raises the average from 6 to 259/43. The source model uses equally likely maximal matchings. Lean verifies the competitor’s tree and diameter, then takes the proposed tree’s optimality from the contradicted hypothesis. Engbers and Erey posed the conjecture in 2023. Sui and Li restated it and proved the diameter-six case. ## 2. Proof of Liu’s Conjecture 1 on Walker’s inequality with side-power weights

**Proof.** Liu (7.3) uses the cyclic factor aᵏ for every real k≤4 and three positive acute sides. The endpoint comparison survives each of the six side orders and equal sides. The proof extends to a right triangle, and the acute equilateral triangle witnesses the hypotheses. The paper knew k=0,1. Its two-parameter questions are broader. ## 7. Disproof of the Aliste-Prieto–de Mier–Zamora conjecture on the smallest trees with the same restricted U-polynomial

**Disproof.** For each cut depth m≥2 the source proposes the least order of an unrooted nonisomorphic pair with equal restricted U-polynomials. ## 10. Proof of George’s Conjecture 1 on nonreal degree roots of trees in the unit disk

**Proof.** For all finite trees, including one vertex, every nonreal root of the degree polynomial has modulus strictly above one. The nonempty open upper half-disk also witnesses nondensity. For example a path has x and x² summands, so the graph polynomial has its usual vertex-degree meaning. The thesis proves restricted degree-two cases. ## 12. Solution to George’s problem on the rational degree roots of graphs of a given order

**Solution to an order classification.** For reduced p,q>0 the classification identifies all orders for which a simple graph has rational degree root −p/q, including the parity factor at even order. ## 13. Proof of the Burstein–Hästö–Mansour conjecture on the packing density of the vincular pattern 12-1

**Proof completing a known claim.** An occurrence is an adjacent ascent followed by a later copy of its first letter. For every alphabet with at least two letters the maximum is ⌊n(n−1)/6⌋. ## 35. Proof of Černý’s Conjecture 34 on counting fair words

**Conjecture proved.** Fairness counts ordered occurrences as scattered pairs. Every nonpalindromic fair word uses fewer than ⌈n/2⌉ letters, so its equality pattern contributes a falling factorial of lower degree. The proof gives one integer polynomial for each positive length, with its prescribed vanishing at 1. At length seven the counts are 20 for two letters and 93 for three. Černý already had the parity condition, the degree bound on the alphabet actually used, and short-length formulas. A subsequent fair-dice construction prescribes equality of order probabilities for fixed dice and does not determine this polynomial. The connected clique-chain example has at least two cut vertices and signs giving one distinct fixed point in each of (1/8,1/4),(1/4,1/3),(1/3,1/2). The general family keeps the two-cut property while producing an arbitrary prescribed number of distinct interior fixed points. Earlier work on inflection points and real reliability roots concerns different equations. ## 102. Correction to the Aldous–Fill hitting-time heuristic for random cyclic adjacent transpositions (Example 7.22)

The step law has m cyclic adjacent transpositions and a hold, all equally likely. Starting and target decks are independent and stationary-uniform. The one-step and two-step return probabilities each equal 1/(m+1) for m≥3, giving the corrected 2/m coefficient; the remaining terms are controlled uniformly to O(m⁻²). The source labels its 1/m approximation a heuristic. Adjacent transpositions on a line and mixing times answer other questions. ## 114. Disproof of Olarte, Santos, Spreer and Stump’s Conjecture 3.5 on dual pairs in flag complexes

**Disproof.** Every ridge of the flag complex is contained in two facets. Enumerating all subsets of the twelve vertices gives 36 edges and 24 triangles. Nine vertices belong to minimal members of the mutually dual row and column families. The octahedron has only six vertices, even before taking essential vertices. The broader EKR conjecture is separate. ## 115. Disproof of Bajnok’s Conjecture 9 on the minimum size of restricted double sumsets in cyclic groups

**Disproof.** The 19 residues modulo 444 give 34 distinct sums of two different elements. Bajnok’s formula predicts 35. Its ordinary sumset term is 36 by the divisor formula. This addresses the numerical minimum in Conjecture 9. The inverse problem in #28 asks which sets attain a minimum. ## 116. Proof of Borg’s Conjecture 1.4 on intersecting systems of signed sets

For every finite, nonempty family of nonempty supports and every alphabet size at least two, the closed Lean theorem constructs a largest intersecting star. It proves that a maximum nonstar exists exactly in the binary case when three distinct coordinates of maximum weighted star degree occur in exactly the same supports. Borg’s signed-family reduction and the hereditary-family weighted theorem in Chang–Liu–Liu, Proposition 5.3, already imply the general size bound. Neither result supplies this equality classification. The audit checked the cylinder argument, the maximizing-star construction, the binary majority witness and the finite ground-set conventions. Exhaustive enumeration of 33,021 small support-family instances agreed. ## 117. Proof of Billey, Fahrbach and Talmage’s Conjecture 6.4 on the complex zeros of peak polynomials

**Proof.** For each nonempty admissible peak set with maximum $m$, every complex zero satisfies both $|z|\le m$ and $\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}z\ge-3$. The common library proves the normalization from permutation counts before applying the polynomial estimates. The authors had checked $m\le15$. Li’s expansion-coefficient theorem already implies the disk bound. The gap induction proves the real-part estimate. The separate last-real-root conjecture concerns a different assertion. ## Separate review of Erdős Problem 708

PR 120 proves the uniform asymptotic upper bound for every interval of the required length. The separate historical branch proves the exact finite inequality in §9 of the 1959 paper. All unordered products of $l$ nearby primes, including squares, give $l(l+1)/2$ inputs for which every covering selection has at least $l(l-1)+1$ elements. A variant uses two disjoint sets of primes to handle every positive input size. For every positive input size it gives an example that requires at least twice that size minus an error of order its square root. The formalization defines the least sufficient budget and proves that its ratio to the input size tends to two. The universal pointwise bound remains open. The historical construction belongs to Erdős and Surányi, who credited Gallai with posing the selection problem. The upper-bound notes distinguish the new sieve estimate from Chen’s, Wang’s and Bergström’s earlier work. The audit checked the quantifiers on the shift, the interval endpoints, the divisibility relation, and the distinction between a limit and a pointwise estimate.
